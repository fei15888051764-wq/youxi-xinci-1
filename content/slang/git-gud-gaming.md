If you've spent any time in Twitch chat, you've seen Git Gud. Probably too much of it.

It's the two-word response to every complaint, every death, every "this game is unfair" moment. And yeah. Sometimes it's genuine advice wrapped in a meme. Sometimes it's just someone being a jerk. Telling the difference is basically a skill in itself. (Irony noted.)

## Why Git Gud Hit Different

The phrase works because it's *almost* helpful. Like, if you strip away the sarcasm, the message is literally "practice more." But nobody says "practice more." They say "git gud," spelled wrong on purpose, delivered with zero empathy, usually right after you whiffed a ward placement and got ganked for the third time.

In [MOBA](/slang/moba-gaming/)s especially, it's complicated. Your [carry](/slang/carry-gaming/) is flaming you for not peeling? Git gud. Your jungler missed a smite? Git gud. Your mid laner got [solo-killed](/slang/solo-queue-gaming/) and pings your roam? Git gud. It's the Swiss Army knife of dismissive responses. Works in any lane, any role, any ELO.

But here's the thing. In Soulsborne games, it's almost affectionate. Elden Ring players say it to each other like a handshake. Because everyone knows the game is brutal. The phrase becomes a communal joke instead of an insult. Context is everything.

## The Origin of Git Gud

"Git gud" is a deliberate misspelling of "get good." The earliest known use traces back to the Metal Gear Online community on PlayStation 3. We're talking late 2000s here. Players used it as a taunt toward newcomers who couldn't keep up with the [meta](/slang/meta-gaming/). It was mean, sure, but it was also efficient. Two words, infinite salt.

From there, it spread like a virus. Dark Souls picked it up around 2011. By 2015, it was everywhere. League, Dota, Overwatch, you name it. The misspelling stuck because it sounds funnier. "Get good" is what your coach says. "Git gud" is what some random in all-chat says after you walk into a bush with three enemies in it.

The Twitch boom in the late 2010s cemented it. Streamers started saying it out loud. Chat spammed it in emote-only mode. It became less of an insult and more of a cultural reflex. Someone complains? Same answer. Someone asks for tips? Same answer. Someone breathes? You guessed it.

## Git Gud in the Wild

**Ranked, minute 28:**

> **support_main:** "I can't peel for you if you keep face-checking jungle entrances."
> **adc_who_died:** "This champ is broken anyway."
> **support_main:** "git gud"
> **adc_who_died:** "..."

Classic. The adc wanted sympathy. They got two words and a lifetime of shame.

Or here's one from a Discord post-match autopsy:

> **you:** "That invade was so free."
> **friend:** "I got CC'd for 4 seconds straight."
> **you:** "Then don't stand there. Git gud."
> **friend:** "You're the worst support I've ever played with."
> **you:** "And yet we won."

The phrase lives in that gray area between banter and bullying. Most of the time, it's just how gamers communicate. We're not great at empathy. We are great at one-liners.

## Common Questions

**Is git gud just another way to say skill issue?**

Pretty much. "Skill issue" is the newer, more clinical version. "Git gud" has more personality. More history. It's like the difference between a hospital gown and a leather jacket. Same function, completely different energy.

**When should I actually say git gud?**

Short answer: when someone's complaining instead of learning. If they're asking genuine questions, don't be that guy. But if they just blamed their team for their own positioning error? Fire away. Just know you might get reported. Worth it? Your call.

**Do people still say git gud?**

Honestly? Yes and no. The phrase itself is vintage at this point. But the *spirit* of it, that "stop whining and improve" attitude, is permanent. You'll still see it in chat, still hear it on streams, still find it in every gaming subreddit. It might mutate, but it's not dying.

**How do I explain git gud to someone who doesn't game?**

Tell them it's what you say when someone blames their tools instead of their technique. Like a chef complaining their knife is dull while using it backwards.
